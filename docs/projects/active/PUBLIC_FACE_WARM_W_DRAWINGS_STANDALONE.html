What this page is for

On 2026-10-23 you ruled that the wordmark is redrawn before the marketing stage (S4), and that the redraw covers the browser-tab mark and the phone home-screen icon as one identity. This page shows today's three marks where they appear, draws three directions in every place a mark has to work, recommends one, and asks you to rule. Nothing is built.

The recommendation: B, the pennant

The chevron becomes a pennant, the flag a team hangs. It keeps today's forward-pointing shape and lime-on-dark tile, so a coach who installed the app still recognises it. It stops being a command-line prompt. The wordmark moves into Barlow Condensed, the face the marketing headlines already use, in two inks. At 16 pixels it is the most distinctive of the three: one solid shape you can pick out of a row of tabs without reading it. A's letters are legible at that size too. They are just two more letters among the tab titles. The argument is on Side by side.

Push back first

These come from the code, not from the plan's description of it.

1 · There are four marks, not three. The ">" is also the notification badge.

The badge a phone shows in the status bar for a FieldLogicHQ notification (badge-72.png) is the chevron drawn as a white silhouette. A coach meets it more often than the home-screen icon, every time a game moves. Every direction below draws it.

2 · Today's home-screen icon is broken, as well as mismatched.

The rule between "F" and "L" runs straight through the L, so the icon reads "F, L struck out". The "HQ" in its corner is navy on black at about 1.5:1, so it disappears at icon size. The maskable version, which Android uses, has no HQ at all. A coach's phone shows "FL" in lime on black, which spells nothing and is the most terminal-looking of the four marks.

3 · "One lime per view" means the mark needs a version without lime

You ruled one lime per view on the marketing pages (Ask 14 A). A lime-filled mark in the bar would be a second lime on every page that has its own. So every direction is drawn twice: a tile for icons, where lime is the fill and the only colour, and a page version in ink and olive for the bars, where lime appears only on Dark. That is a constraint the drawings follow. It does not reopen the lime ruling.

4 · Redrawing the wordmark rewrites two ruled rules, P1 and P9

P1 says the typewriter face is for "the wordmark, a plan chip and real data". P9 fixes the wordmark as IBM Plex Mono in three inks. All three directions take the wordmark out of the typewriter face, because the typewriter is what makes it read as a terminal. That follows from Ask 2 C and does not reopen it. If you rule a direction, S1 writes P1 as "a plan chip and real data" and P9 as the new wordmark.

5 · The bigger risk is the email, not the icon

Email cannot load our fonts, and Outlook on Windows blocks images until the reader allows them. Today the email wordmark falls back to Courier New, so the most-read copy of the name is the most typewriter-like. Each direction below draws its email wordmark in Arial, as text, and I recommend text with no image (Ask 5). B pays for this: its condensed letters become ordinary Arial bold in email, so the two inks carry it there.

6 · Widened: the "FL" icon reaches further than the home screen

The same "FL" file is the large picture on every push notification that has no organizer logo, and the mark printed on every export: the club schedule, the coach money exports and the PDFs. A family meets it on paper more than on a home screen. The redraw replaces it in all three places, and the shipping list counts them.

7 · Found, routed rather than drawn: whose icon a family installs

A tournament page already installs with the organizer's own logo on an iPhone when its plan allows branding. That follows M2: an organization's brand wins on its own pages. The new club site does not. A family who adds their club's page to their phone gets FieldLogicHQ's icon. That is a Club Tier question (the public site builder), not this drawing's, so it is routed there and drawn nowhere here.

8 · The name is written about ten ways, not five

The brief counted five wordmarks. The code has about ten. These include the volunteer check-in and scorekeeper shells, the platform-admin sign-in (two inks, HQ white), the offline page (system sans, not the typewriter), and three copies of the old email header in lime capitals. The admin and coach lockup asks for a weight the typewriter face is not loaded in, so the browser fakes the bold. Whichever direction you rule, S1 should make the name one shared piece that every surface uses, or a redraw becomes an eleventh version.

How to read the drawings

Each direction tab shows the same nine places in the same order: the browser tab, the phone home screen and its crops, the notification badge, the marketing bar and footer, the consumer bar, the admin and coach top strips, email, the share card, and small one-colour use. Marketing is drawn warm only (Ask 5 B). The consumer bar and the operator strips are drawn warm and Dark. The 16 and 32 pixel tiles are rendered at that size and then enlarged, so you see the real pixels and not a sharp vector. Every flag and chip opens its explanation when tapped.

The marks are pure geometry: no font is needed to draw any icon. The wordmarks are drawn here in the real Google Fonts. Where a wordmark has to be an image (the share card), it ships with the letters converted to outlines, so it needs no font either. That respects the no-self-hosted-fonts ruling (2026-10-07). The bars' layout is ruled, so only the mark changes in them, and no navigation is drawn as a proposal.

The recommendation, argued

B, the pennant

  • It passes the bar. A pennant is something a team owns, in every sport: hockey, soccer, baseball, swimming, debate. It says "your team" and not "your terminal". The chevron was a command-line prompt. The pennant keeps its forward-pointing shape and gives that shape a meaning a volunteer recognises.
  • It is the most distinctive at 16 pixels. One solid shape, drawn on the pixel grid so the pole is a clean 2 pixels. A's "HQ" is legible at 16 pixels, and it is the quietest. C's clipboard lines blur into a block. In a row of browser tabs, B is the one you can find without reading it.
  • It changes the least for people who already have it. A lime shape on a dark tile, pointing right. A coach whose phone or browser updates the icon sees a relative of the old one, not a stranger.
  • The wordmark belongs to the page. Barlow Condensed is the marketing headline face (P2). The name in that face, with HQ in olive, looks like part of the page it sits on. A and C use Inter 800 and 700, which is quieter but borrows the reading face for the name.
  • Sport-neutral, with two watch-points. A flag on a pole can read as a golf hole flag. /design found the first drawing did, so the pennant is redrawn long and tapered (about 2 to 1), with only a short pole below it. In North America a pennant also leans a little toward baseball ("win the pennant"). The product's chat already uses a flag icon for Report (Ask 9). If you still see a golf flag or a Report button, that is a reason to pick A, not C.

What B costs. In email, Barlow Condensed becomes Arial bold, so the email wordmark is wider and plainer than the site's. The two inks still carry it. A and C lose less in email because Inter falls back to Arial with little change.

Why not A. A is the strongest in a bar and the most honest about what we are: a name, not a picture. It is the weakest icon. "HQ" on a phone home screen reads as headquarters and says nothing about sport or teams, and at 16 pixels it is two small letters.

Before any mark is committed: nobody has searched for names or logos that are already taken. A search on "FieldLogic" and "Field Logic", and on a pennant mark in sports and forms software, is yours or counsel's to run. /marketing flagged it and could not check it. The more a mark leans on a picture and the less on the generic "HQ", the easier it is to own, which also favours B.

Why not C. The clipboard fits the job: the organized person on the volunteer board carries one. In a row of browser tabs it reads as "paste" or a to-do app, which makes it an office tool and not a team's.
