## What changes for the coach

The printed practice plan stops being a spreadsheet-looking table and becomes a **run sheet** — the
same document a stage manager or event crew carries. Times run down the left edge like a clock;
beside each time sits the block's name, who's running it, and the coach's own notes at full width,
reading like the sentences they were typed as. The rotation chart shrinks to an honest little grid
tucked inside the block it belongs to, with the group names right under it.

Today, a coach who writes real instructions — "Front toss: two-strike approach — choke up, shorten
up, anything close gets a swing" — gets those sentences squeezed into a skinny column and even cut
in half across a page break. After this change, the words the coach wrote are the most readable
thing on the page, which is the point of printing it at all.

## Why it matters

- **The two people holding this paper are served directly.** The head coach follows the clock down
  the edge to run the night; the assistant coaching one station finds their block and sees
  everything they need — instructions, rotation, kids' names — in one place.
- **A normal practice fits on one page.** A packed night takes two, split cleanly between blocks —
  never mid-sentence.
- **It looks like the team's document**: team logo, colour and name up top, honest page numbers at
  the bottom (that plumbing arrives for every PDF in the quality project's first phase).

## What deliberately does not change

- The sheet records what was **planned**, never what happened — no checkboxes, nothing to fill in.
- It stays a hand-carried printout, never a shareable link (it names children with a time and place).
- The player focus-areas section only prints for staff allowed to see focus areas; for anyone else
  it's simply not there.

## Trade-offs made

Two alternatives were rendered and rejected by the owner: tear-apart station cards (great for the
assistant, wasteful of paper and worse for running the whole night — the tear-off idea is parked as
a possible future variant) and a summary-strip-plus-detail layout (the strip duplicated every time
and title and cost an extra page; the run sheet's time column already gives the at-a-glance read).

## Success criteria

- A coach printing a real, note-heavy plan gets pages where every sentence is intact and readable.
- An assistant handed the sheet can point to their station's region within seconds.
- A typical plan prints on one page; no plan ever shows a sentence split across pages or a page
  counter that lies.
