Support Console Stage 5

Stage 5 answers two questions from inside the console: "is the platform healthy right now?" and "what did this customer actually receive?" Today that takes the Amplify, Supabase and email-provider dashboards, the morning check on your PC, and guesswork for any email that is not a campaign. The rule is the one Stage 3 used: the truth is shown when the screen opens, never remembered (R7).

Every drawing is true size, 1440 pixels wide; on a narrower window each scrolls sideways in its own box. Status is also drawn at phone width (your Q4). Before is a capture from today's dev server. After is drawn on that same shell in the product's real warm and dark colours, flipped by the Warm / Dark switch at the top. Blue tags say what changes and why; each opens when you click it. Your nine questions sit beside the drawing each one decides, each with my recommendation, and a paste-back at the end.

One example runs through every drawing: on Oct 26, an administrator of UAT Rep Club (a dev test club) hits an error on the Registrations page, sends feedback about it, and invites an assistant whose address bounces. The person, "Jamie Rivera", and both example.com addresses are made up. No real customer's name or address appears anywhere on this page. Anything marked Stage 6 or 7 is not part of this stage and is not drawn as if it were built.

READ THIS FIRST

Four places where the code or the providers say something different from the prompt (and from my own kickoff)

1 · I am recommending against my own kickoff answer on the morning check.

At the kickoff I recommended that the morning check save its summary where Status could read it. You left that box unticked, and the evidence now says I was wrong. Most of what Status needs to say "is it healthy now?" can be read live by the console itself: our own error and traffic records (kept for a year, so Status gets its own "a usual hour is…" for free), the database's own vital signs, the builds, and the database services. What the morning check adds is yesterday's history and its week-over-week judgement, which are the owner's and the developer's signals rather than support's, plus agent-tooling checks that belong nowhere near customer support. Wiring it in means a new path that writes from your PC to production with an account-wide key, and a 7:30 a.m. reading beside live ones that could look fresh when it is not. Question 2 recommends B: Status does its own live reads, and the morning check stays exactly as it is.

2 · "Read-only keys on prod" is possible for builds, possible-with-a-catch for Supabase, and not needed at all for the database's own vital signs.

Builds: an AWS key can be truly read-only, limited to listing the builds of our one app's two branches. The club web-address key on production is already shaped this way, so there is a precedent. It must not be allowed to open a single build, because a build's details carry a link to its log, and a log can echo settings. Supabase: scoped, read-only tokens exist now (pick the projects, read-only per area). But a token always belongs to a person's account, and every token of that person shares one limit of 120 calls a minute with every agent session that refreshes the snapshots. The database's own vital signs need no key at all: one read-only database function, the same shape Stage 2 used for Find, lets the app ask its own database about connections, stuck sessions and the scheduled jobs. Question 1 lays each out.

3 · Every account email already leaves through one place. Logging them is not the hard part; telling them apart is.

The census found no email that bypasses the shared sender apart from the morning check's own alert. About 80 places send through it: sign-up confirmations, password resets, invites, billing notices, registrations, receipts, and the club's emails to families. The shared sender keeps no message number and writes no record. Only campaigns (which use a different sender) are logged. So the work is: the sender must be told what kind each email is, so it logs account emails and leaves a club's family emails to the club's own log. That makes question 8 smaller than the prompt feared. It also shapes question 6.

4 · The Errors caveat is out of date, and it also needs rewording, not just deleting. Feedback has no page of its own.

591 of 616 routes are wrapped today (96%; the 25 that are not are dev tools, sandboxes and the error collector itself). But the caveat's real job remains: the error rate divides failures by API calls, and errors on pages and in browsers have no call count to divide by. That is why today's screen says 0.00% beside 64 errors. The line should say that, plainly, in one sentence. Feedback is a list with a disclosure arrow; there is no feedback item page to put a conversation on. A reply needs one (a record that reads first, R2), so Stage 5 adds it.

WHAT STAFF AND CUSTOMERS WILL SEE

Six changes, in plain words

  • Status, under Health. One page that reads everything it can reach when it opens and says when it read: the builds, our error and traffic records, the database, the scheduled jobs and the database services. Each line says Fine, Look at it, Act now or Not checked, in plain words, with one thing to do. Readable on a phone.
  • Today's health line points at Status and says what it checked, rather than "builds and the databases are not checked here yet".
  • Errors, restyled. The person an error hit is a link to their record, and the club a link to the club. An event with no signed-in person says so. The caveat becomes one honest sentence, and the clock reads "6:18 p.m.", not "06:18 p.m.".
  • Feedback gets a page, and a reply. Support answers a customer from the feedback item. The reply is an email from us. The window says, before you send, that it cannot be taken back. The customer's answer comes to the admin inbox.
  • Emails, with what happened to them. A person's record gains an Emails tab: every account email we sent them, and what the mail service says happened (Delivered, Bounced, Marked as spam…). An address with no account (an invite that never arrived) is found from Find and shown under Health › Sent emails.
  • The customer: the only new thing a customer ever receives is a feedback reply, written by a person. Nothing else they see changes.

Deliberately not in Stage 5: the morning check's history on Status (question 2); club web addresses and the hosting quota (Stage 6); Campaigns and its own send log (Stage 7, unchanged here); the club's emails to families (the club's own Emails tab, a different log for a different reader, drawing E); answering a customer's reply inside the console (it lands in the admin inbox).

Question 1 · What Status shows, and which lines need new keys

Most of Status needs no new key: our own error and traffic records, the database answering, and, with one read-only database function (a migration, no key), connections and scheduled jobs. Builds need an AWS key that can only list one app's builds, which is a truly read-only key; production already holds one shaped like it for club web addresses. Supabase's service health and gateway errors need a token that is read-only and limited to the two projects. It still belongs to a person's account and shares that account's limit with the agent sessions. That is the one real risk call, and it is yours.

Recommended: all four groups. Our records and the database function ship with the stage. The AWS list-builds key and a scoped read-only Supabase token are your steps at the release; if a separate read-only Supabase member is possible on our plan, the token comes from it. A line whose key is absent says "Not checked: this site has no key for it", never "Fine".

DRAWING B

The morning check on Status: three honest options

The morning check runs at 7:30 a.m. on your PC. It reads the databases and the builds, and keeps its history in a folder there: 38 daily reports since Sep 17. The deployed console runs on Amplify and can never read that folder. What the check knows that a live read does not is history: a 7-day middle for errors, requests, response times and database work; database size against a week ago; advisor findings that are new since yesterday; the heaviest queries' change; an alert email when something turns red; and the Monday digest that proves it still runs. It also checks the agent tooling, which has nothing to do with customers.

Option A

Not at all; Status replaces it

  • Status's live reads become the health check, and the morning check is retired.
  • You lose: all of the history above, the alert email, the Monday heartbeat, the database advisors and the agent-tooling checks. Status cannot alert anyone: it only reads when someone looks.

Cost: retiring watched automation (its own commit, your word). Nothing new built.

Option B · recommended

Status reads live; the check stays on your PC, unchanged

  • Status shows now, from its own reads (drawing A). Errors and traffic get their own "a usual hour" from the counts we keep for a year.
  • The morning check keeps its history, its judgement, its alert and its digest, exactly where they are. Status's foot line says what it does not judge and where that lives.
  • You lose: nothing you have today. Support does not see yesterday's week-over-week judgements; you still do, in your morning report.

Cost: none beyond drawing A. No automation touched.

Option C

The check also writes its summary where the console reads it

  • Each morning the check saves a summary (counts and verdicts, never raw logs) to the production database; Status shows it as one line, "Morning check, 7:30 a.m.: 0 problems, 2 to review".
  • You gain: the history judgements in the console.

Cost: a shared-automation change (its own commit, only on your word, here). A new path that writes from your PC to production, with today's account-wide token or a new write key. It writes only on days the PC is on. And a 7:30 a.m. reading sits beside live ones, so it must say its age loudly or it breaks R7.

Question 2 · The morning check on Status (your Q6, second half)

Status answers "is it healthy right now?" for support. The morning check answers "is it worse than last week?" for you and the developer, and watches the agent tooling. Those are different readers. Recommended: B. Status does its own live reads (drawing A); the morning check stays on your PC exactly as it is; Status's foot line names what it does not judge. This reverses my kickoff recommendation (C), for the reasons in the first box at the top.

Question 3 · Who sees Status, the Errors detail, and the sent log

Status shows verdicts and counts about the platform, never a customer, a log line or a setting, so there is nothing in it to protect from staff. The Errors detail is unchanged. The sent log is the new exposure. A person record already shows that person's address to every role, but the log adds what we sent them, and Sent emails shows addresses that belong to no account (an invitee who never signed up). The roles whose jobs answer "I never got it" are Support (sign-in, invites) and Billing (billing notices). The full table is at the end of drawing G.

Recommended: Status for every staff role; Errors unchanged; the sent log (the Emails tab, Sent emails, Find's group and the Story's email rows) for Super admin, Support and Billing under a new power, absent for Product, Growth and Read only; emails to staff accounts for Super admin only.

Question 4 · The person link on an error

The prompt asked whether the link should reach every role that reads Errors, or only those who can already open people. That is the same set: every role that reads Errors (Super admin, Product, Support) can already open a person record, so a link grants no new view. The real cases are the three blanks: a staff account (only Super admin opens those today), a deleted account, and nobody signed in.

Recommended: the person and the club are links for every role that reads Errors; a staff account links for Super admin only and reads "A staff account" to the others; a deleted account shows its stored email as plain text; nobody signed in says so. If Stage 8 ever narrows who opens people, the link follows the person record's own rule, never its own.

How this differs from the club's own Emails tab

A club already has an Emails tab under Families: what the club sent to its families, with how many families each reached. That is the club's record, for the club's people, and it stays theirs. DRAWING F

The delivery callback: how "what happened" reaches us

Not a screen. When the mail service learns what happened to an email, it calls a new public address on our site. This page is that address's whole life: who calls it, how we know it is really the mail service, what it writes, and what happens when it is called twice or out of order. It also lists the two settings you turn on and the release step it adds.

1We record the email before it leaves

The sender writes our row first (to, kind, "Sent"), then hands the email to the mail service with our row's number attached as a tag. So the callback can never arrive before our row exists. If writing the row fails, the account email still goes (a reset link matters more than its log line), and the gap is counted in the server log. If the mail service refuses it, or this site has no mail key, the row ends as Failed or Not sent at once, never "Sent" for ever. Our own alert emails write no row.

2The mail service calls our new public address

One address, outside the signed-in area, the way Stripe's messages already arrive. It accepts only the events we subscribe to: sent, delivered, delayed, bounced, marked as spam, failed, not sent. Opens and clicks are never subscribed.

3It proves the call is real, before reading anything

Each call is signed with a secret only the mail service and our site know. The signature covers the call's own number, its time and the exact message together, so the time cannot be forged. We refuse a call without all three, a call more than 5 minutes old or from the future, and a message over 64 KB, before reading it. A call may carry several signatures; one match is enough, compared in constant time.

Bad or missing signature → refused, nothing writtenToo old → refusedOur secret not set → "not ready"; the mail service tries again for a day
4It ignores anything it has already seen

Every call carries the mail service's own message number, and a retry reuses it. A number is marked handled only after its change is saved, so a call that failed half-way is applied when the mail service tries again, not swallowed as a copy. Handled numbers are kept 7 days, then pruned.

Same call twice → the second is a no-opFailed half-way → the retry applies it
5It finds our row by our tag and moves it forward only

States have an order: Sent, then Delayed, then Delivered, then the final ones (Bounced, Failed, Not sent, Marked as spam). One database step moves the row only if the new state ranks higher, so two calls at the same instant cannot undo each other. An earlier state arriving late is still added to the email's timeline, but never overwrites. So a "delivered" that arrives after "bounced" leaves it Bounced.

Out of order or at once → the later state standsNo matching row (another site's email, a family or campaign email) → accepted, nothing kept
6It writes three things, and nothing else

The new state, its time, and a bounce reason chosen from a short fixed list (for example "the address does not exist"). Never the mail service's own words or its whole message, which repeat the address and subject. Nothing from a callback is written to the server log.

Question 7 · The callback, or read the mail service live?

Live read instead: nothing stored, nothing public. But the mail service allows 10 requests a second for our whole account, shared with every email we send. Opening an Emails tab of 20 rows would spend two seconds of the budget a club's family send also needs. Its own dashboard keeps only 30 days, so older rows would answer nothing. And when the mail service is down, every row says "unknown". The callback: one migration, one public address, one secret, one setting, one release step. It shows the last known state even while the mail service is down, keeps 90 days, and never competes with sending.

Recommended: build the callback, as drawn, with the release steps above. It is the answer that still works when the mail service is down.

Question 8 · Cover every sender, or the main ones?

The fear was many direct sends. The census found none: every account and family email goes through one shared sender (about 80 calls), and the only thing outside it is the morning check's alert to you. So "everything through the one sender" is every sender. The real work is that each of those ~80 calls must say what kind of email it is, so the sender can log account emails and leave family emails to the club's log. That is what makes the log complete, and a build check can enforce it.

Recommended: the sender requires a kind on every call, and the build fails on a call without one; account kinds are logged, family kinds are not; the morning check's alert stays outside (it is yours, not a customer's).

DRAWING H

What could go wrong, and what stops it

A threat list over the design, before any code. Each line names a way Stage 5 could be misused or could fail, and the rule that stops it. The build turns every rule into a guard or a test (the list below the table).

#ThreatWhat stops it

Tests the build must carry

  • A fake mail service suite. Signatures checked against the mail service's published examples, including a valid old signature with a changed time, and several signatures on one call. The same callback twice → one change. A call that fails half-way, then is retried → applied once. "Delivered" and "Bounced" at the same instant → Bounced. A bad, missing, old or future signature, or an oversize message → refused, nothing written. A callback for no row, or another site's row → accepted, nothing kept, not even its number. Our secret unset → "not ready", nothing written.
  • The reply, against the fake service. A double click handled by two servers → one email. A retry after a lost answer, with the same reply number → one send; with edited words → refused; after a day → refused. A recipient sent from the browser → ignored. Billing, Product, Growth and Read only → refused by the server. An anonymous item, a deleted account, a changed, unconfirmed or blocked address, or feedback over 90 days old → refused. The staff member and an audit entry are recorded. Line breaks in the title never reach the subject.
  • Status's answer has a fixed shape, and is never cached by the browser. Its response holds verdict words, numbers and times only. A test fails if it holds a key's name, a web address, a log line, a commit message or a provider's error text. A missing key → Not checked; a 5-second timeout → Not checked; a late counting job → "may be out of date".
  • The sender. A build check fails on any send without a kind. Family and alert kinds write no row. A stored row holds only an allowed label: a league sign-up for a child named Emma stores no "Emma", a reset email stores no link. No key or a refusal ends the row as Not sent or Failed.
  • Who sees the log. A person opened as Product shows no email rows in the Story. Emails to staff accounts are hidden from every role but Super admin. Every email opened from Sent emails writes a trail row.
  • The database function and the new tables pass the same grant checks as Stage 2's (closed to the public, to signed-in users and to the browser's key; the server only). The function's text never names a job's command, a run's message, or a session's query, user or address.
  • The settings. A build check fails if a new setting is not passed to the server by the build, or is named public or starts with AWS_.
  • Powers. The rulebook test learns the two new powers (reply, sent log). Every new route asks its power before it reads anything. The menu and Find hide what the rulebook hides.
  • Find still never logs what is typed (the A3 guard extends to the Sent emails group and the Sent emails search).
  • The keys. A build check fails if the AWS or Supabase key names appear anywhere a browser can load. The AWS key's permission list is written in the release notes and checked there: list builds, two branches, nothing else.

The security lens

/review's security lens was run by a separate reviewer over this page and the code it stands on, before you saw it. It found 4 high, 9 medium and 3 low issues in the design as first drawn, and every one is now folded into the drawings and the threat list above. The four that changed what you see: (1) the log no longer keeps an email's subject line, because subjects carry club and team names, and on family mail a child's; it keeps a fixed label per kind. (2) The Story's email rows follow the sent-log power, because the Story is open to every role. (3) A reply is refused when the person's address changed after they wrote, or is unconfirmed or blocked, and is offered only for feedback under 90 days old. (4) Status's "once every 30 seconds for everyone" now holds across servers, through the database. The rest tightened the callback's signature check and its de-duplication, banned the database function from reading the scheduled jobs' commands (they carry a secret), kept log text out of everything, put Sent emails lookups in the records-opened trail, hid staff accounts' emails from everyone but Super admin, and named the build step that passes the new settings to the server.
