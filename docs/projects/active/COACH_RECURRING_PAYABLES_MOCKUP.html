What makes the preview trustworthy

  1. The rule travels, not just the list. The form sends the repeat rule and the dates it showed you. The server regenerates from that rule and refuses any date it did not itself produce — so a date you never reviewed cannot be written, and a date you unticked cannot come back.
  2. A ceiling both halves agree on. 24 payments, refused identically by the form and by the server. Monthly across a season is twelve; anything past two years is a typo.
  3. A partial failure names dates. If four of six save, the message says which two didn't — never "✓ done" over rows that aren't there.

D

The one line that decides whether any of this is visible

Load-bearing — a payable with an amount and no due date is not on the schedule at all

A payable's due dates live on its halves, not on the payable. A row saved with a total and nothing else reads "No schedule", never reaches the Payment schedule tab, never counts in Due next or the next thirty days, and offers no Mark paid — it is a commitment with no date attached, and the screen says so honestly.

E

Repeats and the deposit/balance split can't both be on

Undefined together — so the form states the rule instead of the server discovering it

Both groups write the same two dates. "Monthly, and also split into a deposit and a balance" has no obvious meaning — does every month get both halves, or does the split spread across the series? Neither answer is right, so opening one closes the other, with a word saying why. The values you'd typed are kept, so switching back and forth loses nothing.

Rule Paid rows are frozen — on the server, not just on screen. A request that names a paid occurrence is refused outright. Money that has moved is not a scheduling operation.

What is stored, and what it will never do

The run remembers its own shape — its cadence, its day of the month, its dates — so "Add more months" knows what "more" means, and so any occurrence can find its siblings. Each payment is still a complete, ordinary payable: editable, deletable, and markable paid on its own, exactly as if it had been typed by hand.

The remembered rule never creates anything by itself. Nothing is written unless a coach opens this sheet and confirms a list they can see. That was the point of the original "no series" decision, and it survives intact — what changed is that the coach can now come back to the list instead of hunting twelve rows.

One-off edits aren't undone by a series edit

A payment edited on its own — say February was set to $0 because the club covered it — is marked edited in the sheet. A whole-run re-price shows what it would do to that row before it does it, so a deliberate exception isn't quietly flattened by a bulk change.

F

Left out on purpose

Not in v1

"This and all future occurrences" as a question. The sheet in G shows the actual payments instead — simpler to use, and safer over money that has already moved. There is still no rule quietly generating rows in the background.

Weekly and biweekly payables. Monthly is what the money actually does — dome and field blocks, insurance, equipment financing.

Per-occurrence descriptions ("Winter dome block — Nov 2026") and suggesting a repeat from a budget line. The first is unnecessary on a date-ordered schedule; the second is a different feature.

The dependency

This ships after Edit and Delete for payables. The series sheet is a convenience over rows that must each be fixable on their own — that base has to exist first. The other change on this screen, the single Add button with an Expense/Payable switch, means Repeats is absent on the Expense side: an expense is money already spent, and repeating it means nothing.
