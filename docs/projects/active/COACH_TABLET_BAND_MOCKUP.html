The 641–768 band

Six coach screens had never been measured. Adding them returned 318 findings — every one a control too small to hit, none of them at desktop width. This page is what those controls look like at true tablet size, and the one decision that resolves most of them.

👉

Open this page on your iPad Every control below is real, at its real size — not a picture of one. Put a thumb on it. A tap target judged on a laptop has not been judged. The frames are pinned to 768 px and will not reflow; on a narrower screen, swipe them sideways.

Every finding is the same kind — a control shorter than 44 px. No sideways scroll, no unreadable text, nothing broken at desktop width. Strip out the repetition and the 318 are 58 distinct controls. But they are not one problem, and this is the part the brief did not have:

  • 21 of those controls fail only between 641 and 768. They are correct on a phone and correct on a laptop, and wrong in the middle. 207 findings.
  • 37 of them fail on a phone too. That is ordinary touch debt on screens nobody had measured — the same pile the portal already carries 173 of at each phone width. 111 findings.
Worth saying out loud

The tablet band is the smaller half by control count and the larger half by finding count. Answering the tablet question does not finish these six screens — it finishes two of them.

03

The question is narrower than it looks

The brief asks whether a twelve-player, six-inning lineup grid is a touch surface. That reads as an open question. It is not — the product answered it, built the answer, and ships it every day on a phone.

Below 640 px the grid already becomes a touch surface: every position cell is raised to exactly 44 px, the 18 px drag handle and the 22 px remove ✕ are removed from the grid entirely, the player's name is pinned so it stays visible while the innings scroll, and reordering and removing move to the Batting order tab — where both controls are already 44 px, at every width, today.

None of that applies at 641 px. A coach on an iPad gets the mouse version of a screen the product has already decided is a finger screen. The decision on the table is not what a lineup grid is. It is where that decision's edge belongs.

One thing not to change

The words. The check keys every recorded decision on a control's accessible name, so renaming “Preview” orphans its entry and quietly loses whatever was decided about it. Sizes move; labels do not.

Specimen 4 · One whole screen

The lineup builder, at true 768 px, before and after. Not a drawing — the real screen, rendered twice this morning, with the phone arrangement's rules applied at 768 for the second shot. This is the one that shows the cost, and the cost is two rows.

Two fewer rows before a scroll, on a screen a coach scrolls anyway — a twelve-hitter lineup never fitted on one screenful at this width. Nothing a coach opens the builder for moved below the fold: the score, the warning, the format and innings controls, the auto-fill tools and the three view tabs all sit exactly where they were.

08

The recommendation

B · Move the touch edge to 768 Recommended

Because the product has already made this decision once and got it right. Shipping arrangement A at 768 while arrangement B runs at 640 means asserting that an iPhone has fingers and an iPad has a mouse, with the boundary at 640 px — which is not a claim anyone would defend if it were written down.

Cost, measured not guessed: the grid grows 586 px → 730 px, the page 1,352 px → 1,496 px, the width does not change at all, and two rows move below the fold. Return: 109 sub-floor controls → 13 on the builder, 108 → 12 on the template. Roughly 190 of the 318 findings close on one breakpoint.

C · Exempt the grid as a pointer surface

Defensible only if the answer to “can a coach use the lineup builder on an iPad?” is “no, and we will say so.” That is a real answer and cheaper than B — but it puts the product in the position of shipping a between-innings tool that does not work on the device it is most likely to be held on. If you pick it, the exemption goes into the check with a name and a reason, never into the baseline.

Move the check's own floor below 768

I would not. It erases 417 recorded findings in one line, which is exactly why it looks attractive, and it is contradicted by the product's own stylesheet: fourteen rules in the coaches sheet already treat 768 as a touch width. Changing the floor would not settle the disagreement, it would hide it.

What this proposal deliberately leaves alone

  • The shared help “?” — 34 px on all five screens, and on every other screen in the portal. Standing precedent from the commitment page: shared chrome lands on every screen at once or it is not the fix.
  • The 698 unexplained baseline entries. None of these 318 get written into the baseline. Closing them by recording them is how the record starts lying.
  • The six screens' place in the sweep. They stay listed. Being absent is the defect this inherited.
