Helper or assistant?

A helper

A parent or an outside instructor who turns up to run a station. One screen, read-only, no staff chat. Perfect for the person doing the tee for an hour.

An assistant coach

Staff. They start with the schedule, attendance, lineups and the roster, they’re in your staff chat, and you can grant more from their card.

Both sign in as themselves — you never share a password. You can promote a helper to an assistant coach later without inviting them again, and take any of it back at any time.

Third copy of one explanation — the radios say it, the card on the left says it, the rail says it. The grid’s own hints are three words each.
Inviting is permanent and first. It happens two or three times a season; the people sit under it every visit.
continues ≈ 2,300px further — three more people, then the phone stacks the rail underneath
What the frame shows
  • The people are below the fold. Invite card + access card ≈ 640px before the first name. On the two screenshots you posted, the first staff card is the third screen down.
  • Every person is a full control panel. Eleven controls each, so the list cannot be scanned — it can only be scrolled. A head coach with three assistants has no view that answers “who can see the money?” in one look.
  • The head coach is not in the list. It is an assistants list with a helper section, not the team’s staff.
  • Invites vanish once sent. There is no “invited, not yet accepted” state, no resend and no cancel. The only place a pending invite can be seen is the club admin’s oversight page — which a standalone Premium team does not have.
  • A helper’s card has no controls. To give a helper attendance you must first “Make assistant coach” (which also hands them schedule editing, lineups, documents and the staff chat), then switch four things back off — and the card then relabels them “Assistant” because the word is worked out from the switches.

What changed and why
  • The people come first. The list is the page. Inviting is one button in the header, where every other page in the portal puts its page-level action.
  • One row per person, one line of access. Chips name the areas in the same words the sheet uses. Amber chips are the sensitive ones, so the scan you actually do — “who has money, who has contacts?” — takes one glance.
  • Role is a label you chose, not a word worked out from the switches. A helper you also let mark attendance stays a helper. See §04.
  • The explanation lives in one place — the sheet, beside the control it explains — instead of three. The page keeps one sentence and a link to the help article.
  • Pending invites exist. Row, resend, cancel, and the starting access is editable before acceptance — which is what makes “set access first, then send” safe to trust.

Decisions inside this frame
  • Four starting points, not two. “Team manager” is the parent who runs money, forms and family email and never touches a lineup; “Team treasurer” is the parent who keeps the books and nothing else.   • The role is a dropdown, and it is the app’s existing one: each option carries its sentence, so the choice is explained where it is made. The house rule says a one-value field is a dropdown, with radio cards reserved for choices that cannot be changed afterwards — and this one can, from the same sheet. unchanged control
  • Nothing is preselected. The field opens on “Choose who they are”, and the access section beneath stays a quiet note until a role is picked — the same cold-open shape the money screens use.   • One confirmation, at send. All sensitive grants in the invite are confirmed together, once, in one sentence that lists what is being handed over. Today each one is a separate dialog after acceptance.

Decisions inside this frame
  • Role is stored, never derived. It chooses the starting bundle, the word on the row, and the email wording. It gates nothing — every door still opens on the individual grant, which is the safety property the original “preset, not a role” decision existed to protect. Keeping the label while the switches move is the one thing a derived label cannot do.
  • Moving between roles is one pick, any direction. The Role dropdown applies that role’s bundle; a widening confirms, a narrowing does not (a head coach taking access back is always in a hurry — the current rule, kept).
  • Sensitive stays a visible group, not a fold. In a sheet there is room; a collapsed group with a “2 granted” count was a fix for a card that was already too tall.
  • Consequences are said on the control. “Turning this on also opens the roster page” is the one rule today’s grid never states, and the one a head coach cannot guess.
  • “Make head coach” exists here — confirm, then this person can do everything, including manage staff. Today a standalone Premium team has no way to change its head coach at all; a club team needs the admin. new

Why the list-and-sheet wins
  • Most staffs are one to three people. A matrix earns its density at six or more; below that it is a spreadsheet with mostly empty cells.
  • Fifty cells that each confirm. Every sensitive cell needs its speed-bump dialog, and a mis-tap in a grid of forty is easier than in a sheet of ten.
  • No room for the sentence. The matrix has nowhere to say what “Documents” means or that attendance opens the roster page — the explanations the owner asked for have no home here.
  • It does not survive a phone. Head coaches manage staff from the field.
  • The chips give the matrix’s one benefit — a per-person scan with the sensitive ones marked — without its cost. If a club ever runs a ten-person staff, a read-only “Compare” view of this shape can be added as a door from the list.
