The Overview's budget tile counts unpaid bills as spent

Pre-existing, found by the review — not part of the dues build. Every money surface in the portal counts only dollars that have actually moved: the Money hub's Cash card says so on its face, Budget vs. Actual matches it. Except one — the team Overview's budget tile adds up every logged expense at face value, paid or not. Log a $1,600 tournament payable you haven't paid yet and the two screens disagree on sight — and the tile can flash "over budget" before a dollar has left the account.

The same team, the same moment. One screen says over budget, the other says $1,450 of room. Both are defensible numbers; showing them under near-identical labels is the defect.

Option A — make the tile paid-only Recommended

Matches the doctrine every other surface follows; the two screens above become one story. Trade: committed-but-unpaid money stops pressuring the tile — but that's exactly the job "What's coming up" already does, one screen away.

Option B — keep the number, fix the word

The tile says "$8,150 committed" instead of "spent." Honest, zero behavioral change. Trade: still disagrees numerically with the Cash card, and "committed vs. budget" is a subtler idea than the tile has room to explain.

Option C — show both

"$6,550 spent · $8,150 committed." Complete. Trade: the Overview's one-thing-per-tile ruling exists precisely to stop tiles carrying two numbers.

Season refunds: what belongs in the pot, and can it over-promise?

The Season Refund Calculator takes a hand-typed "total remaining funds," gives each player their individual credits off the top, then splits the rest evenly. Two soft spots, both predating the payment record:

  • It can promise more than the pot. If credits across the roster add to more than the number the coach typed, every credit is still shown paid in full — the sheet quietly promises money that isn't there.
  • All credit types count the same. A fundraiser rebate (the player's own earnings), an overpayment (real cash the books hold), and a discretionary "contribution" credit all flow into refunds identically. Arguably right — a credit is a promise to that family — but it's never been decided deliberately.
! Credits owed back ($1,450) are more than the funds you entered ($1,200). These refunds promise $250 you haven't got. Check the amount — or remove a credit that shouldn't be returned.
↳ Your books show $1,310 cash on hand right now — use this figure. Includes $60 of overpayment credits that the Collected figure deliberately leaves out.

Option A rendered: a warning that only appears when the promise exceeds the pot, plus a one-tap suggestion sourced from the team's own books — so the hand-typed number stops being unassisted guesswork.

Option A — guard and guide, keep the model Recommended

The warning + suggested figure above. The credit-first refund model stays exactly as designed. Small build, no new concepts.

Option B — let the coach choose which credit types return

Checkboxes: include fundraiser / overpayment / contribution credits in refunds. Maximum control. Trade: a settings surface on a once-a-year screen, and every combination is a new way to surprise a family.

Option C — leave it alone

Coaches doing season-end math usually know their pot. Trade: the over-promise render above stays possible, and it's the kind of sheet that gets screenshotted to families.

Who can read a payment's note?

Recording a payment takes an optional note. Money access and family-privacy access are separate grants — an assistant can be allowed to see the numbers but not guardian names and contacts. A free-text note sits outside that fence: "from Dana's account, she'll send the rest Friday" is visible to any money-view assistant, guardian name and all. (Expense and payable notes have always worked this way too — deciding this decides those by precedent.)

Amounts, dates and methods stay visible either way — only the free text is in question.

Option A — keep notes visible, stop inviting names into them Recommended

The note field's example text currently suggests "from Dana's account" — it teaches coaches to put guardian names there. Change the example to something neutral ("paid at practice") and add a one-line hint. Cheapest, consistent with every other money note. Trade: relies on the coach's judgment.

Option C — payment notes join the privacy fence

The render above: notes hidden from viewers without the family-access grant. Cleanest boundary. Trade: inconsistent until expense/payable notes follow, and a money assistant loses genuinely useful context ("cash, gave receipt").

Option B — both

Neutral examples now, fence later across all money notes in one decided sweep.
