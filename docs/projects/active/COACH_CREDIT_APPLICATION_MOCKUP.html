Fundraising Pays the Bill

A fundraising credit today lowers a player's total balance — but no installment knows it. The plan keeps asking for the full amount, reminders chase the gross figure, and the credit does its work off to one side where only the balance column can see it. The owner's model fixes the altitude: a credit lands on real installments, last bill first, and the amount a family is asked to send drops the moment the money is earned. It also hands season's end its missing distinction — a credit that lowered a bill has been spent; one that never got the chance is money still owed back. From there the season-end refund stops being a calculator and becomes a settlement sheet that is true all year (§7).

Call 1

Cover the bill, or rewrite it?

Everything below covers the installment (its face amount survives; the credit is shown paying it down). The alternative — editing the installment's amount — is argued against under §1.

Call 2

Three ways credits can meet the bills — who chooses?

One team-wide setting in Money settings, three options: last payment first (drawn as default) · next payment first · keep separate — credits never touch installments and settle at season's end. Under §2.

Call 3

Do hand-entered credits behave the same?

Proposed: yes — one rule for every credit (fundraiser, contribution, other). Overpayment credits need no rule: they only exist once everything is covered, so there is never a bill left for them to lower.

Call 4

Does the family hear about it?

Drawn in §4: reminders quote the lowered amount and say why — the fundraising earned it. The alternative is a quietly smaller number with no story.

Call 5

Is a parent's out-of-pocket expense a credit?

Drawn in §5: an expense marked "a family paid out of pocket" counts in the budget as usual, moves no team cash, and creates a reimbursement credit — one mechanism, not a parallel system.

Call 6

Does money-out ride with this build?

You asked whether to cover it here. Proposed: yes — design it now, build it in the same passes. A payout is the receipt book's mirror image, and the season-end refund is just the final, bulk payout — it can't be designed without this.

Call 7 · RULED

Does a family that still owes get a share?

Yes — and the money they owe counts as coming in. The pot grows by the debt, everyone's share rises, and their share cancels most of what they owe. Worked in §7.

Call 8 · RULED

Who is counted in the even split?

Owed-back follows whoever earned it (active or departed — it's their money); the even share goes to the season's-end roster. Anyone left out raises everyone else's share.

Call 9 · RULED

What if the team can't cover what it owes?

Say so plainly, share nothing, pro-rate nothing. Today's screen promises the money anyway. Under §7.

Call 10

Forgiving a balance, and setting an amount by hand

You asked for both. Proposed: one rule serves them — a forgiven balance is money that family already received, and a hand-set refund is a fixed amount off the top. Everything left splits among the rest. Under §8.

The receipt book already settled half of this: payments fill installments oldest-first. Credits take the other end — newest-first — so the two never fight over the same dollar. Cash keeps the season on schedule; fundraising eats the bills at the far end, exactly the way you described it.

Call 1, argued. The credit could instead rewrite the installment — edit $800 down to $300. That reads simpler and fails three ways: delete or resize the credit and the product has forgotten what the bill was; regenerate the schedule and the edit is silently lost or double-applied; and the books can no longer answer "what were the dues, and how were they met?" — which is the exact question season's end is about to ask. Covering keeps the plan a plan and the credit a fact, the same separation the receipt book just paid for.
2

The moment a credit is born

Nothing new for the coach to operate. Recording a fundraiser entry already creates the rebate credit; the sheet simply gains the same "Where it lands" preview the payment sheet has, so the coach sees the bills drop before saving. The two sheets now speak one language: money in shows where it lands, whichever door it came through.

New: the preview block and the plain-language consequence strip. Unchanged: the entry form itself, the rebate arithmetic, the team-share ledger posting, and the standing ruling — a rebate lowers that player's dues and is never team income.

Call 2, argued. One team-wide choice in Money settings — "Credits reduce: the last payment first · the next payment first · they don't — settle at season's end" — applied to every credit alike. Last-first is the drawn default: it protects the season's cash rhythm (near-term installments keep their dates and amounts; the far end shrinks). Next-first gives relief now to the family that needs it now. Keep separate is for teams that want dues to be dues: bills never move, reminders chase face amounts, and every credit dollar arrives at season's end as owed-back (§6) — today's behavior, made honest. A per-credit picker was considered and dropped — it turns every fundraiser entry into a decision, and two credits pointing different ways on one player is a story no one can retell.
3

When the credit outgrows the last bill

A big drive can earn more than the final installment. The credit simply keeps walking forward: it finishes the last bill, then starts on the one before it. Same rule, no special case.

Where it lands — $900.00 earned
Installment #4 — due Mar 31 −$800.00 fully covered by fundraising — nothing to send
Installment #3 — due Jan 31 −$100.00 was $800.00 to send — now $700.00

New: the cascade. An installment fully covered by fundraising reads "Covered by fundraising", deliberately not "Paid" — Paid stays cash, so the books can always say which was which. If the credits ever cover everything still open, the player's row reads Settled and reminders stop entirely.

4

What the family is asked for

Reminders are the only dues figure a family ever sees. Once a bill can be partly earned, the reminder quotes what's genuinely left to send — and says where the reduction came from, which is the best sentence a fundraising program can put in front of a family.

Today — the credit is invisible

$800.00 due Mar 31 (Installment 4 of 4)

…sent to the family whose bottle drive already earned $500 of it.

Proposed — the earning shows up where it counts
To the Ledger family Dues reminder — QA Season End U15

$300.00 to send of the $800.00 due Mar 31 — $500.00 covered by fundraising New

New: the earned line and the net figure, in all three reminder paths (send-now, 30-day, 7-day) — riding the remainder machinery the receipt book just built, so part-paid and partly-earned compose: a bill can read "$100.00 to send of $800.00 — $200.00 received, $500.00 covered by fundraising." Removed: a fully-covered installment can no longer be chased at all — it has a $0 remainder, and $0 remainders are never candidates. Call 4: whether the reminder names the fundraiser or just shows the lower number.

5

Money going the other way

A credit is really money the team owes a family — and lowering their bills is only one way to pay a debt. Two more doors, both drawn here: the coach hands the money over in cash, or the debt is born from a family covering a team cost out of pocket. Same concept in every case; only the settlement differs.

New: the payout sheet — the receipt book's mirror image (amount · date the money left · method), opened from the player's drawer or the credit row. A payout can be partial; whatever remains keeps covering bills (or keeps waiting, under "keep separate"). Remove a payout and everything re-derives, same as removing a payment.

New: the "Paid by" choice on an expense (Team stays the default) and the reimbursement credit it creates. Unchanged: everything else about expenses — the category joins Budget vs. Actual exactly as if the team had paid, because the cost is real either way. What changes is only the cash story: nothing left the team's account, so the books stop pretending it did. Call 5: the reimbursement is an ordinary credit (new provenance, same lifecycle) — it follows the team's credits setting, can be paid out, and lands in season's end if still open. No parallel system.

Why this belongs in this build (Call 6, argued). Once credits can lower bills, "the coach paid the rebate out in cash" must exist — otherwise the product's only story for that everyday act is a wrong one (bills stay lowered while the family also holds the cash). And the season-end refund is exactly this payout sheet run for every family at once: designing the refund without it would re-invent it badly. Proposed sequencing: credits-on-bills first, payouts + out-of-pocket second, season's end third — three passes, one model.
6

What this hands season's end

Your sentence is the whole basis: "if a credit lowers a payment it has effectively been earned already." Once credits live on bills, every credit dollar is in exactly one of two states, and the product can finally tell them apart without a coach typing anything:

  • Applied — it lowered a bill; the family sent less cash. Spent. It owes nobody anything at season's end.
  • Paid out — the coach settled it in cash along the way (§5). Also spent, with a ledger line to prove it.
  • Owed back — it never found a bill to lower and was never paid out (the family had already paid, or the team holds credits separate). The books are holding this family's money.

7

The refund sheet — no calculator, no button

The Calculate button goes away, and with it the typed pot. What replaces it is a section at the foot of Player Dues that simply shows, at all times, what the team owes each family — and it is honest year-round, not just in April. A family holding an unapplied rebate in October is owed that money in October.

First: where the pot comes from

New: the whole card — the pot is derived and shown working, never typed. Removed: the "Total Remaining Funds" input and the Calculate button. The only control left is hold back — stating an intention ("keep $500 for next year's equipment") rather than re-doing arithmetic. Hold-back can only ever come out of the surplus: a coach cannot hold back money the team owes families, and the field is capped at $1,525.00 with that reason given.

A real trap this replaces — and it is the reason the pot must be derived. The figure in circulation for this team was $1,575, reached as dues + the team's 75% share of fundraising − spending. But the books receive the full $2,500 raised; the players' $625 is a credit, not a deduction from income. So $1,575 had the rebates already removed — and today's calculator then subtracts all credits again, taking the same $625 out twice and understating every family's refund. Cash held is $2,200. A number a human types is a number that can be wrong in a way no screen can see; a derived one shows its work, which is what the card above does.

8

Forgiving a balance, and setting an amount by hand

You asked for two controls. They turn out to be one rule: some amounts are settled before the split, and whatever is left divides among the families still taking a share. That keeps the promise the whole sheet rests on — the rows always re-add to the pot, exactly.

Forgive a balance

A family isn't going to pay the rest. Forgiving writes it off — and because the team has genuinely given them that money, the forgiven amount counts as their share, already received. One rule, and it behaves sensibly at both ends:

ForgivenTheir benefitCash to themEveryone else
$20.00$154.50$134.50$154.5010 shares
$200.00$200.00$0.00$169.449 shares

Forgive a little and they still get a cheque, just a smaller one — the $20 is part of their $154.50. Forgive more than a share and they take no cash at all; the $1,525 then divides among the remaining nine at $169.44, which is exactly the outcome you described. Nothing special-cased: the same sentence produces both. The pennies that don't divide land on the last row so the total still reads $1,525.00.

New: the whole sheet, opened from the row. The deliberate limit: a hand-set amount replaces a family's share and never their owed-back money — that is theirs, not a distribution the coach can redirect. And whatever a set amount frees up redistributes immediately, so the sheet cannot be left holding an unallocated remainder. Why not a free-text override on the refund total: it would let the rows stop adding up to the pot — the exact defect deriving the pot just removed. Every control here is bounded so the total is always true.

One phrase covers all of it. Owed-back money is paid to whoever earned it. Amounts already settled — forgiven or handed over — count as that family's share. Whatever is left divides evenly among the families still taking one. Debt joining the pot (Call 7), a departed player stepping out (Call 8), forgiveness and set amounts (Call 10) are all that same sentence, which is why the sheet can always show its work and always balance.

What this deliberately leaves alone

Totals don't move

The rolling balance already subtracted credits; this changes where the reduction lives, not how much it is. Every roster total reads the same before and after.

Credits still aren't income

A rebate lowers that player's dues; the team's share was income the day the drive closed. The standing double-count ruling holds untouched.

One credit mechanism

No new credit types, no second overpayment path. Automatic overpayment credits keep their existing life; they simply show up in the owed-back column, where they always belonged.

The receipt book's rules

Payments are still facts, still fill oldest-first, still post to the ledger dated the day they arrived. Cash always claims a bill before a credit does.

Everything re-derives

Change the dues, remove a payment, resize a credit, undo a payout — where credits land is recomputed the same way payment coverage already is. Nothing is stamped, so nothing goes stale.

Live seasons only

Credits move money. Nothing here opens in an archived season, and the archive allow-lists are not touched.

The dues screen keeps its shape

The refund sheet is a section at the foot of Player Dues, not a new tab. Every roster column above it keeps its current meaning.
