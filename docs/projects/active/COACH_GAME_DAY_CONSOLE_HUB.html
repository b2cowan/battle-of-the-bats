The Bench Console Re-Draw

The game-day screen, measured at the fence and redrawn top to bottom. Ruled, BUILT and REVIEWED, 22 Sep. Two defects fixed, the score demoted, scouting promoted, the board editing like the builder — and nine more found by /review, including two the fixes caused themselves.

Drawn, built, reviewed 22 Sep 2026
Measured before and after, 390 and 360
Read from the browser on dev — never a screenshot

What this is

The bench console is the one screen in this product a coach holds at a fence, one-handed, in daylight, with a game going on. It shipped in three passes last month and was never re-read at phone width — the phone re-evaluation covered the shell, the Overview, the Schedule and the lineup builder, and stopped there. This is the console’s turn.

Measuring it turned up two things that are not design preferences. Every button on the action bar is covered by the phone’s navigation bar and cannot be tapped — including End game. And every player already on the field is a dead control. Both are reproducible; the evidence is on the next tab.

Re-framed by the owner, 22 September: this screen is for the things a coach updates in the dugout — game notes, scouting and lineups. Scorekeeping is not one of them, and with the live site parked a running score updates nothing. Tracing it confirmed the call has room to spare (F10): the mid-game score feeds a glance and a pre-filled field, and nothing else in the product reads it. So it comes out, the inning stepper takes the pinned slot, and scouting gets promoted out of a dotted underline.

What a coach sees change

  • The buttons come back. Who’s here, Note and End game sit above the navigation bar instead of underneath it, so they work from the moment the screen opens.
  • You can tap anybody. Tap the position beside a player’s name — on the field or on the bench — and the same list of positions opens that the lineup builder opens. Moving a player who is already on the field from second base to shortstop stops being impossible.
  • A third of the wasted space comes back. The strip along the top goes; the arrival time and kit stop taking two lines; and only the score and the inning follow you down the page instead of the whole header card. Nine players on screen instead of six.
  • The “Saved” tick stops living at the top. It becomes the pill every other coach screen uses: it appears when something is saving, says Saved, and fades. Only a failure stays put.
  • The screen just stays on. The chip goes. Pressing the power button still sleeps the phone, and the phone still sleeps in your pocket — both of those are the handset, not us.
  • The word “Home” stops appearing twice. The matchup line already says it — vs for a home game, @ for an away one — so the separate word beside it goes.
  • The back arrow loses its word on a phone and keeps going where it already goes: this game’s entry on the schedule.
  • Scorekeeping comes off the screen. The big running score goes; a quiet Update score row under the bench opens the same score sheet if you want it. Nothing about the record changes — the final score is still entered when you end the game, exactly as it is today, and that is what families are sent.
  • Scouting gets a door you can see. Today the opponent’s book is reachable only if you happen to know the opponent’s name is tappable. It joins Who’s here and Note in the bar, because it is one of the three jobs this screen is actually for.
  • The sideways “Full grid” table goes. Fixing the fifth inning while you stand in the second is now the inning arrows — the same one-inning-at-a-time read the builder was given yesterday.

Who is affected

Head coaches and assistants who run the bench. A helper with schedule-only access sees the same board read-only and is unaffected except that their buttons become reachable too. Nothing here touches the tournament-scored game, the read-only recap the same address serves after the game, or anything a family sees. No new data is recorded, no migration, no route learns a year.

Priority and success

The two defects are a hotfix — they make the live screen unusable for its one job, and they are cheap. The redraw is a normal stage, worth doing in the same pass because it touches the same header and the same footer.

Success: a coach can end a game without scrolling; a coach can change any player’s position in one tap from the row they are looking at; nine of eleven players read on one screen; and the console stops being the one coach screen with its own save word.

What the screen does now

Every figure below was read out of the browser against the UAT fixture, at the two phone widths the portal is held to. Nothing here is estimated from a screenshot.

F1

Every button on the action bar is covered by the navigation bar

Defect

Who’s here, Note, Full grid and End game are drawn underneath the phone’s navigation bar and cannot be tapped. They surface only when the page is scrolled to its very last pixel. The bar pins itself to the bottom of the window without accounting for the navigation — the console is the only screen in the portal using that bar, which is why nothing else caught it.

F2

Players on the field cannot be tapped

Defect

Every row under On the field is a disabled control unless a bench player has already been selected. Tapping Avery does nothing. The only live thing on the board at rest is a bench row, so the flow only runs backwards — bench first, then field. If both players are already on the field, there is no path at all: the change can only be made through the dropdowns in the sideways-scrolling Full grid sheet.

44% of the screen is spent before the first player’s name

High

At 390 the first row starts 369px down an 844px screen, and six of eleven players are visible. At 360 it is worse, because the inning arrows drop onto a second line under the score and the header card grows by 52px. And the whole 216px header card is what pins as you scroll — the matchup and the kit chips follow you down the page along with the score.

F4

The “Saved” tick is the old, retired pattern

High

The console hand-rolls its own save word and pins it in the top strip, always visible. The portal settled this on 20 September: the word is a floating pill that shows nothing at rest, appears on an edit, says Saved, lingers a couple of seconds and fades — and only a failure persists. Five screens render the shared piece. The console is a sixth autosaving screen that was never added to the guarded list, which is precisely how it was missed.

F5

Two ways to change a position, neither of them the builder’s

Medium

The console asks you to select a bench player, then tap who they go in for. The lineup builder — redrawn for phones yesterday — asks you to tap the position beside a name and pick from a sheet. Same data, same save, two grammars. The third way is the Full grid sheet: a sideways-scrolling table of small dropdowns, which is the only route for a field-to-field move.

F6

“Screen staying on” costs a whole line of the header

Medium

The chip is a 44px-tall button sitting among 26px chips, so the chip row wraps to two lines. It was built because a screen that refuses to sleep without saying so reads as a broken phone. That reasoning no longer holds on its own: the power button sleeps the handset whatever a web page asks for, and the lock already lets go when the tab is hidden. The honesty belongs in the help article, not in the most crowded screen in the portal.

chips row 74.8px at 390 (two lines) → projected ~37px without the chip
F7

The back arrow names the page, not where it goes

Medium

The destination is already right — it returns to this game’s own entry on the schedule, with the sheet open. The label says Schedule, which describes the page it lands on rather than the thing you go back to, and it spends a third of a 47px strip saying it. Every other action on a phone in this product is icon-only.

F8

The “GAME DAY” badge announces which screen you are on

Low

A coach who opened the game-day screen knows they are on the game-day screen. What the badge is genuinely carrying is live — that this game is inside its window and writes are open — and that is worth keeping in a much smaller form, because the same address serves a read-only recap outside the window.

F9

The screen says home-or-away twice

Low

The matchup already encodes it — the product writes vs for a home game and @ for an away one — and then a separate word Home sits on the right of the same line saying it again, in a different grammar. Found while drawing the replacement, not while measuring: the four chips would not fit one line, and the reason the fourth existed was that this word had nowhere else to go.

F10

The running score is the biggest thing on the screen and nothing reads it

High

Owner ruling, 22 September: the scorekeeping comes out. Tracing what depends on it says the call is safe with room to spare — the running score has exactly two consumers, and neither is the season record.

what the mid-game score feeds the header glance the block itself End game’s inputs a pre-fill you type over the season record nothing — a mid-game result is refused by design the schedule / insights nothing — they read a FINISHED game the recap’s W 6–4 badge nothing — drawn by its own block

The result is captured at End game, which has its own two score fields and its own win/loss/tie badge. A coach can also record a score from the schedule without ever opening this screen. So the header block buys a glance, and costs the most valuable 50px on the page.

SETTLED

Scorekeeping comes off the screen — owner, 22 Sep

The running score block goes; a quiet Update score row under the board opens the same sheet. Checked before drawing it (F10): nothing in the product reads a mid-game score, the season record refuses one by design, and End game keeps its own two fields. Nothing is lost that is not one tap away at the moment you actually want it.

G1

What shape is the board?

You asked for the lineup editor’s experience. That splits into two readings.

A — the console keeps its board and learns the builder’s controls Recommended

On the field and Bench stay, with the bench still longest-sitting-first. Each row’s position becomes a real button opening the builder’s position sheet. The select-then-tap swap stays as a second route for “put Casey in for Avery”. Full grid goes.

B — the console’s board becomes the builder’s list

The same one-inning-at-a-time list the builder renders: batting order, neighbouring innings in small type, the number as a drag handle. One component, one grammar, nothing to keep in step.

Why A. The board answers “who is out there right now”; the builder’s list answers “what is the plan”. At a fence the first question is the whole reason this screen exists, and B’s batting-order list cannot answer it without the coach reading eleven rows. A gives you the part of your ask that matters — the edit is one tap and it is the same sheet — without trading away the read. B is defensible and cheaper to maintain; if one component matters more to you than the read, it is B.

G2

Does the console keep a permanent action bar?

A — keep it, fix the clearance, drop to three Recommended

It sits above the navigation bar. Full grid leaves with the sideways table, so Who’s here, Note and End game get their labels back a size. Cost: 139px of permanent bottom chrome.

B — no bar at all

Who’s here becomes a fact you tap in the header (“11 here · 1 out ›”), Note and Scouting join the board’s foot beside Update score, and End game is a full-width button at the end of the document — which is where you are when the game ends. Reclaims 67px.

Why A. Big targets you can hit without looking are the reason the bar was built, and that was right. The problem was never that it existed — it was that it was invisible. B is tempting but makes End game a thing you go looking for.

G3

How is the opponent’s scouting book reached?

You named scouting as one of the three dugout jobs. Today it has the weakest affordance on the screen: a dotted underline on the opponent’s name, which a coach finds only by tapping a word that does not look like a button.

A — a door in the bar, and keep the name too Recommended

Scouting takes the slot Full grid vacates. The opponent’s name keeps its underline as the contextual route — it already works and costs nothing.

B — the bar only

The name becomes plain text. One door, no chance of two routes drifting apart, and the matchup line reads quieter.

Why A. Two routes to one sheet is only a problem when they can disagree; these cannot. The bar makes it findable for the coach who has never found it, and the name stays right for the coach already looking at the opponent. Worth knowing: after the game the recap already offers scouting as a prominent line — “while it’s fresh, add to the book?” — so the book is well served after and invisible during. This closes that gap.

G4

The wake lock — silent, or still disclosed?

A — silent, disclosed in help Recommended

The chip goes. The screen stays on while the game is live and lets go when it ends. The help article’s paragraph is rewritten to describe the behaviour rather than the control.

B — keep an off switch somewhere quieter

Moved off the header entirely — into the tools row, or a line in the game sheet.

This is the one I’d flag. A standing ruling says a screen that refuses to sleep without saying so reads as a broken phone. I think A is still right — the power button sleeps the handset regardless of what a web page asks, and the lock already releases in your pocket, so nothing is actually trapped — but this retires a ruling, and that is your call, not mine.

Build plan

One screen, one stylesheet block, one help article. No migration, no new route, no year parameter, nothing a family sees — and the score-writing paths (the quiet mid-game write, End game, the schedule’s own score form) are untouched behind their door.

Part 1 — the two defects

Shippable on its own, ahead of any ruling.

  • The action bar clears the navigation. It reads the same clearance value the save pill, the docked bar and the sidebar already read, instead of pinning to zero, and its stacking rises above the nav. The console is that bar’s only consumer, so nothing else moves.
  • Players on the field become tappable at rest. The disabled condition goes; a tap on a field row opens that player’s position sheet. The existing select-then-tap swap keeps working when a bench player is already chosen.

Part 2 — the header

  • The top strip is deleted. The back control becomes an icon-only 38px button in a 44px target inside the card’s first line, keeping its word above phone width; it returns to where the console was opened from, defaulting to this game’s sheet on the schedule.
  • GAME DAY becomes a small live dot and word on the matchup line; the read-only recap keeps saying so in its own words.
  • The hand-rolled save word is deleted and the shared pill rendered instead. The console joins the guarded list of autosaving screens, so the next time that word moves this screen moves with it.
  • The wake chip and its switch go; the lock itself stays, still live-window-only and bench-coach-only.
  • The running score block is deleted (F10). The card splits: matchup and chips scroll, and the pinned slot goes to the inning stepper with the builder’s status dots — the same piece, so the two screens step through innings identically. The arrows are 36px inside 44px targets — the phone-door precedent from stage 2 — so 360 stops wrapping.
  • A quiet Update score row sits under the board, opening the existing score sheet untouched. End game keeps its own two fields and its win/loss/tie badge — the result is captured there, as it already is, and the family notification is unchanged.

Part 3 — the board

  • Each row’s position becomes a button opening the builder’s position sheet. Same component, same cell edit, same save, one undo step — nothing new is recorded, exactly as the console’s founding constraint requires.
  • The bench keeps its frozen longest-sitting-first order and its streak chips; the field keeps its pitch-count and absence warnings. Neither is in the builder’s list, and neither is lost.
  • Full grid and its sideways table go, and Scouting takes the slot. Correcting a later inning is the inning arrows — the builder’s own answer. The door to the full builder stays, for batting order, modes and caps.

Verification at build

  • Re-measure every projected figure at 390 and 360 and write the real numbers into the plan — read from the browser, never a screenshot.
  • Re-run the hit-test: all three action buttons reachable at scroll 0, mid-scroll and at the page end.
  • The layout sweep’s console screen, the tap floor and the field floor; the phone guard; the save-pill guard with the console added to its list; the selector check.
  • A helper with schedule-only access still gets the board read-only with no write controls, and their buttons are reachable too.

Help and docs

One article changes: the game-day section’s “Who’s here, and keeping the screen awake” loses the chip and describes the behaviour; its search terms keep screen keeps turning off and phone keeps sleeping, which is what a coach actually types. Full grid and screen staying on leave the search text. Plan, PM brief, TODO and the QA ledger are stamped in the same unit of work.
